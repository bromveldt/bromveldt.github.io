#### Trusts and shell companies are transforming America into a tax haven for the super-rich

Charlotte Gifford

14 August 2023 • 9:00am

Where do the super-wealthy hide their riches? The white sandy beaches of Bermuda probably spring to mind, or else the private banks of Switzerland.

In fact, the world’s most affluent are increasingly piling their wealth into American states, some with a population smaller than Devon.

As the financial systems of infamous offshore tax havens have become more transparent, the US has grown into the biggest enabler of financial secrecy in the world.

That is according to claims from the Tax Justice Network’s latest Financial Secrecy Index, which ranks jurisdictions by their “complicity” in helping wealthy individuals conceal their finances.

The US came out on top for the first time in the most recent Financial Secrecy index published last year, overtaking infamous tax havens such as Switzerland, the Bahamas and the Cayman Islands.
The index ranks countries based on how much financial secrecy they supply to the world, rather than the scope for financial secrecy their systems allow. Pa example, Vietnam has a financial secrecy score of 81 – but it ranks 24th on the index, owing to its relatively smaller influence on the world stage.

Overall, the Tax Justice Network estimates that the US costs the world $21bn (£16.5bn) in lost tax.

This is all thanks to the pro-anonymity loopholes introduced by its states in order to feed a fast-growing wealth management industry and woo the world’s elite across their borders, experts say.

#### The Great Plains tax haven

Best-known for the national monument Mount Rushmore, South Dakota is a sparsely populated state in the American Midwest – close in size to Romania but with fewer than 900,000 residents.

Yet this unassuming state has enjoyed a huge influx of domestic and internal capital in recent years – flooding not into the local economy, but into its trusts.

Casey Michel, author of American Kleptocracy: How the U.S. Created the World’s Greatest Money Laundering Scheme in History, claims these trusts in South Dakota have “become the new favourite tool of cartel heads, fentanyl traffickers, crooked oligarchs, and more – and hundreds and hundreds of billions of dollars have raced into South Dakota in recent years as a result”.

According to South Dakota’s annual report, the state currently holds $600bn in trust assets – a huge rise of 21pc year-on-year. At that rate, Mr Michel estimates that assets stored in trusts will hit $1 trillion by 2030.
Trusts have been around for centuries, and enable individuals to transfer assets such as property outside of their estate to another party, a trustee, who manages it on behalf of a beneficiary.

So what is the reason for the South Dakotan trust industry’s rapid expansion? The answer is favourable tax laws and deregulation, experts say. There is no state income tax in South Dakota, and trusts can be held secretly.

On top of this, if you put an asset in a trust, it will stay there, virtually untaxed, forever. This is because, in 1983, South Dakota became the first state to abolish the Rule Against Perpetuities – caps on the lifetime of trusts – paving the way for so-called “dynasty trusts” that help settlors preserve their wealth for generations.

Chuck Collins, of the Institute for Policy Studies think tank in Washington, says that since the 1980s, as the US “wealth defence” industry has grown, it has been successively persuading legislators in South Dakota to pass laws that make trusts highly attractive to the super-rich.

“The trust industry says ‘this will create jobs! All you have to do is change the law, make trusts anonymous, make them last forever, and we’ll attract all this global capital’,” he says.

“You have to remember, South Dakota is a small state. So adding 500 or 600 jobs to the trust industry is pretty compelling. That’s a lot of people going out, eating at restaurants, putting money into the local economy. And these are highly paid jobs.”

As soon as deregulation began in one state, it quickly spread to others, he adds.

Since South Dakota abolished it, more than half of US states have either weakened or repealed the Rule Against Perpetuities, according to analysis by the IPS – facilitating the growing use of dynasty trusts across the country.

Michel says there has been a “race to the bottom” as states try to attract the super-rich by offering as much anonymity as possible.

Alaska, in 1997, passed a law allowing the creation of domestic asset protection trusts. This means someone setting up a trust can also be the beneficiary, allowing the person to shield their assets from creditors and tax authorities. Many states have followed suit, including Nevada, South Dakota and Delaware.

#### The states where the wealthy are hiding their money in trusts

Trusts are not the only vehicle the super-rich use to sidestep tax. “The past few decades have seen US states becoming world leaders in creating anonymous shell companies – corporate vehicles that allow anyone, anywhere, regardless of their criminality or source of income, to effectively anonymise their wealth while still controlling their funds,” Michel says.

Part of the problem, he claims, is that unlike American bankers, American lawyers have never been subject to any anti-money laundering requirements. “US attorneys are completely free to work with as much dirty money as they’d like, and to inject it directly into the American economy – and make as much money as they’d like along the way.”

The International Consortium of Investigative Journalists (ICIJ) created such a window in 2021 with the publication of the Pandora Papers, which shone a light on how the wealthy use trusts in the US to avoid tax. The investigation identified 206 US-based trusts with total assets worth over $1bn. Of these, 81 were in South Dakota, more than any other state.

Nearly 30 held assets connected to people or companies accused of fraud, bribery or human rights abuses, the ICIJ found.

The growth of the trust industry may have facilitated job growth in US states – but it has come at a huge cost to the rest of the country and the world, reports suggest.

Last September, the IPS published the report Billionaire Enabler States, intended to highlight the harm caused by this shadow financial system.

It claimed states such as South Dakota and Nevada were allowing the theft of revenue from the world’s poorest nations, shifting the wealthy’s tax burden onto everyone else and fuelling inequality.


#### ‘Inertia’ on improving transparency

It may seem surprising that the US has climbed up the Financial Secrecy Index while offshore tax havens around the world have come under intense scrutiny as a result of leaks such as the Panama Papers.

The development of the Common Reporting Standards (CRS) in 2014 marked a major shift in global tax transparency. By signing up, countries agree to automatically exchange financial account information – making it harder for individuals to conceal their wealth offshore without the rest of the world knowing about it.

Before this, countries generally had to request this information from other nations.

Moran Harari, of the Tax Justice Network, says: “The US is the only country in the OECD that has not signed up to the CRS. The US has never agreed to join and is unwilling to.”


Instead the US has its own system – the Foreign Account Tax Compliance Act (FATCA). But while this allows it to identify assets held offshore by US citizens, it does not allow other nations to see how much international wealth is stockpiled on its own shores. “This is the main hurdle to transparency,” Harari says.

Despite this, Michel adds, there are signs of improvement. “The US will soon be rolling out a new corporate formation database, identifying who is actually controlling shell companies around the country,” he says. “It will be a huge step in the right direction to ending the US’s role as the centre of offshoring, at least as it pertains to anonymous shell companies.”

However, he adds, “there’s much more that needs to be done”.

Collins adds federal trust reform is needed to curb state laws, make these vehicles more transparent, and stem the flood of hidden money into the US, but says there is currently “inertia” from lawmakers.
