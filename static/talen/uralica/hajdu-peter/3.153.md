**Author:** Eugene Helimski

**Hajdú, Péter,** b. Dec. 27, 1923, Budapest, Hungary, d. Sep. 19, 2002, ib.; Hungarian linguist, one of the most prominent figures in international Finno-Ugric scholarship in the second half of the 20th century, who achieved fundamental results in Samoyedic linguistics and in the comparative and historical research of Uralic languages.

H.’s interests were concentrated on “minor” Uralic languages and their history since his college days (Budapest University, 1941–45, under the guidance of J. Györke, Gy. Laziczius, M. Zsirai). After six years of work at the Széchényi National Library, he continued his academic career as head of the Finno-Ugric Department of the Institute of Linguistics of the Hungarian Academy of Sciences (1951–59) and later as director of this Institute (1974–82), professor of Szeged University (1959–74), professor (since 1982) and chair of the Finno-Ugric department (1986–1991) of Budapest University. Elected corresponding member in 1972, ordinary member in 1976 of the Hungarian Academy of Sciences, he was for many years in charge of the Language and Literature section of the Academy.

Until the mid-20th century there was no Samoyedology in Hungary. H. not only wrote a handbook *The Samoyed Peoples and Languages* (1949, 1963) and a chrestomathy with Nenets and Selkup data accompanied by an exhaustive bibliography (1968), but also cared — especially during his Szeged years — for the institutionalisation of Samoyedologic research and for securing its continuity. His special studies (e.g., 1950 and numerous later articles and congress papers) deal with phonology and historical phonetics, morphology (esp. etymological morphology) and syntax, historical lexicology, and external contacts of all Samoyedic languages. Acute observations on language phenomena, which were partly reflected already in the data collected by M.A. Castrén but never received an adequate explanation (e.g., the demonstration of the mora-counting principle in the Nganasan language) go side by side with broad typological comparisons in which the Samoyedic data are presented in areal perspective, collated with the data from Finno-Ugric and from Siberian languages, in development of R. Jakobson’s idea of a Eurasian *Sprachbund* (cf. 1975b).

Using the data of etymology in combination with those of archaeology, palaeobotanics, etc., H. developed the theory of the original proto-home of the Uralic language family (in Western Siberia, partly also on the European side of the Ural mountains) and the chronology of its gradual disintegration (1964) which remain the cornerstones of further research. His studies in this field meant also an important contribution to the methodology of palaeolinguistics. H.’s proto-home theory, with its careful argumentation, stands in sharp contrast with the arbitrary equilibristics with facts and pseudo-facts which is characteristic of the contemporary “root-finders” who are seeking roots of the Uralic languages in palaeolithic Europe.

Other domains of H.’s permanent interest were the history of Finno-Ugric studies in Hungary and of their international context, the Uralic etymology (esp. in connection with the issues of ethnogenesis, cf. [1953]), the study of folklore. For many decades, he attentively followed and reviewed new non-linguistic publications, esp. from Russia, evaluating them from the viewpoint of their potential relevance for Uralic studies.

More than anybody else, H. contributed to the successful transformation of traditional Finno-Ugristics into contemporary Uralistics, which not only admits the genetic relationship between Finno-Ugric and Samoyedic languages but also makes ample use of the Samoyedic linguistic and ethnologic data, and which is open and eclectic enough to be receptive to any useful innovations in theory and methodology. Most of the Finno-Ugrists who are active today can consider themselves to be, at least indirectly, H.’s pupils. His handbooks of Uralic studies (1962, 1965, 1966, 1981 with numerous partly revised re-editions) serve as standard manuals at the universities in Hungary and, being translated into English (1975a), German (1987), Russian (1985), Polish (1971), Italian (1992), are widely employed in the Finno-Ugric linguistic education all over the world; besides, they are used as models and sources of inspiration for new handbooks and courses. The popularity of these works is conditioned, in particular, by the author’s reasonable and well-proportioned approach in which the synchronic analysis is combined with the diachronic one, the data of linguistics with those of adjoining disciplines, the material of Uralic languages with the theories and methods developed in linguistic typology and areal linguistics.
