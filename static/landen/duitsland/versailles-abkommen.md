the German economy did not suffer from the Versailles treaty - read A.P.B Taylor

Tony Judt

The author refers to Peterson's "trouble deciding whether Nazism was ideologically 'radical right' or 'radical left,' suggesting that 'maybe they pulled from the worst of both extremes.' This, too, is built on a massive ignorance of the historical research on these topics."

It's worth noting that noted historian Tony Judt, too, suggested that German extremists moved back and forth from radical left to radical right.

I recommend that Nilsson read Volker Ullrich's biography of Hitler (Volume II should be available in September). Ullrich is able to make clear, as few writers have, that Hitler, while guilty of unprecedented crimes, was not the troglodyte that most modern voices feel compelled to make him. He unquestionably had exceptional ability; it was the failing of of his enemies to grasp that. In particular, Hitler had facility making decisions rapidly in the light of imperfect information. He was an inspired improvisor. Where he failed most deeply was in his assumption that only modern European history counted. He drew most of his parallels from German history of the two centuries before his time. This made him discount Russia and the United States as major historical actors, and that in turn led him into what was by far his greatest strategic error, invading Russia.
